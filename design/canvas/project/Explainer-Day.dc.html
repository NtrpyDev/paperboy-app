LIGO's two detectors caught a ripple in spacetime lasting a fifth of a second. Its shape matches black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses spiraling into one.

Key idea

The first direct detection of gravitational waves, and the first sight of two black holes merging.

The question

Can a machine on Earthfeel space stretch?

A gravitational wave is a ripple in spacetime. Before this paper, no one had detected one directly.

When a wave passes, lengths change. Here a 4 km arm changed by far less than the width of a proton. Could any instrument register something that small?

What they did

Two detectors,each an L with 4 km arms.

LIGO has two detectors, one in Hanford, Washington and one in Livingston, Louisiana. Each is an L-shaped laser interferometer.

On September 14, 2015, at 09:50:45 UTC, both recorded the same signal. It reached Livingston about 7 milliseconds before Hanford. The event is named GW150914.

What they found

One chirp,a fifth of a second long.

The signal swept up in frequency from 35 Hz to 250 Hz in about 0.2 seconds. A rising sweep like that is called a chirp.

Its shape matches two black holes spiraling into one, about 410 megaparsecs away, roughly 1.3 billion light years.†

† The mass and distance values carry wide error ranges. The distance, for example, is 410 megaparsecs with a range of roughly 230 to 570.

The two black holes had about 36 and 29 solar masses. The one they formed has about 62. About 3 solar masses of energy left as gravitational waves.

By the time the wave reached Earth, its peak strain was 1.0 × 10−21.

Why it matters
